Overview

TL;DR A frozen audio language model can search an hour-long recording by itself. It asks of every 20-second window whether it helps answer the question, then listens again to the best minute, with no transcript and no training.

Meetings, lectures, broadcasts and podcasts last an hour or more, yet the answer to a question often sits in a few seconds of them. Many audio language models cannot take an hour of audio as input, and those that can must still find those seconds. A common fix transcribes the recording first and searches or reads the text. That makes the hour fit, but it throws away the sounds, voices and timing that audio models are built to hear. Systems that return to the audio instead must be trained for it, again for every new model.

ReListen reduces the search to many small decisions the model can already make: does this short excerpt help answer the question? One frozen model reads each yes/no decision as a probability, ranks every window of the recording, and then answers from the highest-scoring minute, labelled with its times. We fix the configuration on half of LongAudioSpan and evaluate it unchanged on the other half, on VoiceGiraffe and on LAT-Bench.

Method

ReListen uses one frozen audio language model, the reader, both to search and to answer. It cuts the recording into 20-second windows every 10 seconds. For each window, the reader hears the audio with a prompt built from the question: Does this audio excerpt contain information that helps answer the question? Answer yes or no. No answer is decoded. We read the next-token distribution at the answer position instead.

The score of window w_i is the log-odds of a yes, with c the yes/no prompt and p_\theta the reader's next-token distribution:

\ell_i = \log p_\theta(\text{yes} \mid w_i, c) - \log p_\theta(\text{no} \mid w_i, c)

We call this a Jev-style scan, since like the Jev decision model it answers a typed yes/no question with a probability instead of text. Each window costs one forward pass, and all windows share the prompt and the length, so they batch without padding. The cost grows linearly with duration at constant memory, and the windows are independent, so the scan divides across GPUs.

ReListen then takes the windows in decreasing order of score, keeps those that do not overlap a window already kept, and stops at three, that is, 60 seconds of audio. The reader answers the original question once from these excerpts in order, each labelled with its start and end time in the recording. For a grounding query it returns one interval instead: the best window, extended left and right through consecutive windows with positive scores.

Results

The reader is Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct with frozen weights and greedy decoding, and the same reader answers from every input, so differences reflect only what it receives. LongAudioSpan has recordings of 10 to 164 minutes, VoiceGiraffe 123 recordings of 35 to 79 minutes, and LAT-Bench 1,002 grounding queries about recordings of up to 30 minutes.

The scan finds the evidence

With the same 60-second budget, the scan's minute overlaps the annotated evidence for 69.2% of LongAudioSpan and 88.5% of VoiceGiraffe questions. Transcript retrieval with BM25 or BGE-M3 reaches at most 47.8% and 58.8%. The gap is largest on questions about sounds and voices, which leave no words to match. There, transcript retrieval falls to 45.6% and the scan keeps 76.5%.

One selected minute answers better

On VoiceGiraffe, answering from the selected minute is the most accurate input. It beats uniformly spaced excerpts by 21.3 points, the whole recording by 10.6 points with 44 to 54 times less audio, and a transcript of the whole recording by 9.2 points. It is also ahead of the two systems that reason over timestamped transcripts. On LongAudioSpan, the same minute beats uniform excerpts by 8.5 points.

It grounds events without training

For a grounding query, the expanded region of the scan reaches 47.7 mIoU on LAT-Bench without any change, on par with LAT-Audio, which is trained for temporal grounding and refines a global timeline with audio crops. The scan also finds the right region more often at an IoU of 0.3, and it grounds a query in 3.1 seconds, against 173 seconds for LAT-Audio.

Faster than reading a transcript

On LongAudioSpan, ReListen answers a question in 17.3 seconds on one GPU. The text-mediated systems take 27.6 to 1,124 seconds, since they first transcribe the whole recording. On both benchmarks ReListen is seven to eight times faster than the reader's transcript pipeline. Its peak memory stays below 68 GiB at every duration, whereas the full recording exceeds one 80 GB GPU beyond about 62 minutes. Because the windows are independent, four GPUs make the scan about three times faster with the same selections.

Analysis

The evidence is worth hearing, not only reading

To test whether the selected minute should be heard rather than read, the reader also reads its own transcript of exactly the excerpts it would hear. On questions about what is said, listening and reading differ by about two points or less. On VoiceGiraffe questions about sounds and voices, listening is ahead by 8.6 points on the scan's excerpts and by 19.5 points on the annotated evidence, since a transcript discards what these questions ask about.

Where the scores peak

Across a recording, most windows score below zero, and the score rises sharply where the question's evidence is. In the lecture below, three separate passages score high, and the highest of them falls on the narrow stretch the benchmark annotates.

Counting occurrences, with a supervised extension

Some questions ask about the k-th occurrence of an event, such as a sound planted in the recording. Every occurrence matches the description equally well, so relevance alone cannot tell them apart. For these questions we study a supervised extension. Two small acoustic detectors, trained on synthetic edits, propose candidate times, the scan adds its best windows, and a calibrated model gives each candidate a probability p_j of being real.
