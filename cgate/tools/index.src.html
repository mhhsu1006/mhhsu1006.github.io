Overview

TL;DR C-Gate feeds speech to a language model as sparse convex mixtures of the model's own frozen text embeddings. The same fixed vocabulary carries both what was said and how it was said.

A speech LLM needs a bridge from a speech encoder into the language model's input space. Some bridges are tied to the vocabulary through objectives such as CTC or text alignment, which emphasise semantic content, while emotion also depends on prosody. Others produce free continuous vectors, so where those vectors settle relative to the text embeddings is an outcome of training rather than a property of the design.

C-Gate separates the two choices. For every pooled speech frame it scores the full vocabulary, keeps 16 rows of the frozen embedding table, and mixes them with non-negative weights that sum to one. No projection follows the table, so every bridge output lies inside the convex hull of the text embeddings. The task losses alone decide which rows are selected and how they are weighted. No transcript-token target guides the selection.

With a Whisper-large-v3 encoder and a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct decoder, one checkpoint trained jointly on speech recognition, emotion recognition and spoken reasoning reaches 3.98% and 8.42% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other and 84.3% emotion accuracy on test recordings from eight corpora, with one decoder and no emotion classifier. Because every output lives in shared embedding coordinates, the selected tokens, their weights and the decoder's attention can be inspected and intervened on directly.

Method

Convex Gate

Let \mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d_{\mathrm{llm}}} be the frozen embedding table. Every bridge output is restricted to its convex hull,

\operatorname{conv}(\mathbf{E})=\Big\{\textstyle\sum_{v=1}^{V}a_v\mathbf{E}_v \;:\; a_v\ge 0,\ \sum_{v=1}^{V}a_v=1\Big\}.

From a pooled encoder state \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t, C-Gate forms a query and scores every row of the table against a learned key projection, then keeps the largest k=16 entries:

\mathbf{q}_t=\mathrm{LN}\big(f_q(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t)\big),\quad \mathbf{K}=f_k(\mathbf{E}),\quad \boldsymbol{\pi}_t=\mathrm{softmax}\Big(\frac{\mathbf{q}_t\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_p}\,\tau}\Big),\quad S_t=\mathrm{TopK}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_t)

The selected probabilities are renormalised and used to mix the original rows of the table:

\alpha_{t,v}=\frac{\pi_{t,v}}{\sum_{u\in S_t}\pi_{t,u}}\ \ (v\in S_t),\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{e}}_t=\sum_{v\in S_t}\alpha_{t,v}\,\mathbf{E}_v\in\operatorname{conv}(\mathbf{E}).

The key projection enters only the scores, so it changes which rows are selected and never the vectors passed on. There is no value projection and no MLP after the mixture. With 152,064 rows and a scoring dimension d_p=512, the scorer has 2.5M parameters. The encoder output is mean-pooled with stride 4 before scoring.

What the bridge carries

Because the bridge lives in the decoder's own embedding coordinates, each part of it can be inspected separately: the mixture weights, the identities of the selected rows, and how the decoder attends to the sequence.

The weights are almost uniform

Within each 16-row support, the renormalised weights are close to uniform for every model and every input task: normalised entropy is 0.960 to 0.990, and C-Gate's bridge vectors have a mean cosine similarity of 0.94 to 0.95 with the plain average of their 16 rows. Replacing the weights with exactly 1/16 changes emotion accuracy from 84.29% to 81.63% and WER from 4.01% to 5.85%. The weights matter a little, but most of the information is elsewhere.

The selected tokens carry emotion, but do not name it

A logistic-regression probe that sees only how often each of the 1,000 most frequent rows appears in a recording's supports, with no weights and no order, reaches 63.3% emotion accuracy against 57.6% for mean-pooled Whisper states, and beats the Whisper probe in all ten speaker-disjoint partitions (Figure 1).

The code is not lexical. On the 84,663 frames of the emotion test split, C-Gate selects 19,997 distinct rows, and 43 of them account for half of all selections. The most frequent are word pieces unrelated to the input, which occur in nearly every recording. A row that spells an emotion label appears in the support of 70 frames, 0.08% of the total.

The decoder reads it differently per task

For each example, the attention from the first five answer tokens to the audio frames at decoder layer 14 is summarised by its effective rank, which is at most five. When transcribing, the models trained on speech recognition reach 4.43 to 4.47: each answer token looks at different frames. For the emotion answer, the joint models stay at 2.71 to 2.87: the answer tokens share one attention pattern over the clip.
