Overview

TL;DR Inside a speech LLM, speech becomes geometrically close to text in the middle layers, yet the late layers still form the answer differently. Alignment is not the same as using the representation the same way.

End-to-end speech LLMs often answer a question less accurately when it is spoken than when it is written, even when the spoken content matches the text. A common way to study this modality gap is through representation geometry: measure how closely speech representations align with text, and push them closer.

Geometry gives the gap a measurable form, but it does not show whether the model uses aligned representations in the same way. We connect the two views. For the same questions given as text and as speech, we compare cross-layer Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) with layer-wise answer margins in four open-weight speech LLMs on SpeechMMLU and VoiceBench BBH, and we edit attention inside the model at fixed input to test what drives late-layer answer separation.

  • GeometryIn Qwen2.5-Omni and MiniCPM-o, speech and text align in a broad middle-layer band.
  • FunctionTheir late-layer answer margins still differ, so mid-layer alignment does not imply functional equivalence.
  • MechanismAt fixed input and token count, sharpening late speech attention increases answer-code separation in all four tested conditions.
  • Two failure modesQwen2-Audio shows weak alignment instead, so the gap takes different forms across models and calls for different repairs.

The gap

Both benchmarks are semantics-matched: the answer is determined by the linguistic content, not by speaker identity, emotion or prosody. Every model is less accurate from speech, by an amount that depends on the model and the task. In Qwen2.5-Omni the BBH gap comes mostly from Web-of-lies, a truth-chain task where accuracy falls from 89.6% to 52.0%.

Geometry: speech and text align in the middle layers

We extract hidden states for the same item as text and as speech, align speech frames to text tokens with dynamic time warping at one base layer, and compute linear CKA between every speech layer and every text layer. Three regimes appear along the speech layers. Early layers are not close to any text layer. In the middle layers similarity rises into a broad band, so several text layers match the same speech layer about equally well. On SpeechMMLU each speech layer aligns with roughly six text layers near its peak. We call this broad band Semantic Smearing. In late layers, consecutive speech layers often share the same best-matching text layer.

The middle-layer band is not an artefact of interpolation or pooling. Pairing each text token with a single native speech frame, the lift in CKA from early to middle layers and the band width both stay above their position-permutation baselines for both models on both benchmarks (paper Table 2a).

Function: answer margins diverge in the late layers

At the decision position we read each layer through the model's final normalisation and vocabulary head and track the margin between the correct answer and its strongest competitor. Grouping the 1,000 BBH items by which route answers correctly, the route that is correct has the larger margin in the late window, layers 23–28, in both discordant groups.

Averaged over layers 23–28, the text margin is larger by 0.135 for the items only text answers correctly, and the speech margin is larger by 0.230 for the items only speech answers correctly. The difference between the two, the crossover contrast, is +0.365, and it stays positive for full-vocabulary probabilities and signed logits. Because the groups are defined by outcomes, this locates where the routes differ. The attention edit below tests a cause.

Late attention allocation causally affects answer separation

Speech and text also differ in how attention is spread at the decision position. Across every head of Qwen2.5-Omni on 100 SpeechMMLU items, speech needs a median 2.20× as many tokens as text to cover 90% of the attention in layers 7–21, and 3.74× in layers 23–28.

Decision-position attention of one head for a single SpeechMMLU item (paper Figure 5). The speech panel uses a ten-times smaller vertical scale. Dashed lines mark the fraction of tokens that holds 90% of the mass.

Attention weights alone do not establish causal use, so we edit them. With the input and token count fixed, we sharpen decision-position speech attention in layers 23–28 while preserving each head's total mass on the speech span, and measure the change in the absolute gap between the two answer codes, Δ∣zX​−zY​∣. The answer-to-code mapping and the order of the rule instructions are counterbalanced.

The gap takes two forms across models

Qwen2.5-Omni and MiniCPM-o reach a clear middle-layer alignment band with text while their late answer margins depend on the route. We call this Decision Instability. Qwen2-Audio has weak cross-modal CKA through the middle of the network, with row maxima of at most 0.07 on SpeechMMLU and 0.13 on BBH in speech layers 10–21. We call this Representation Failure. LLaMA-Omni shows broad similarity with little layer-specific correspondence. The two patterns point to different targets: representation formation when alignment is weak, and late answer separation when alignment is present.

Compression repairs a subset of speech-only errors

As a diagnostic, we compress the speech signal or its token sequence and count how many of the items that text answers correctly and speech answers incorrectly are repaired. Temporal compaction of the waveform and token merging inside the model each repair a subset of these errors, and their relative advantage depends on the evaluation protocol.
